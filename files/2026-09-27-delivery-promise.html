What delivery time should a Dubai food app promise?

On a Dubai weeknight, promising the model's P90 earns 25.1 AED more per 100 checkout sessions than a kitchen-plus-ride formula (16% more), and does better on 19 of 20 paired nights. In rain the formula keeps promising dry-road times: 20% of its orders arrive more than ten minutes late, against 1.1% under P95.

Dubai Marina, JLT and Barsha Heights: 442 restaurants and 823 residential buildings on 20,431 road nodes from OpenStreetMap. 20 simulated nights per evening type; every policy sees the same customers, kitchens and road delays.

The experiment already in your logs

Apps round the promise up to five minutes. Two checkouts whose model estimates are 29.9 and 30.1 minutes are alike in everything that matters, but one is shown 30 minutes and the other 35. Comparing checkouts just either side of each step is a regression discontinuity: it measures what five promised minutes cost from logs a platform already has, with no A/B test, whose two arms would share the same riders. The one thing to add to the logs is the estimate before rounding.

To test it, the simulation logs 4,000 weeknights (6,440,395 checkouts, 1,595,461 orders) of a platform promising P80, in a world built to fool a dashboard: busy hours bring both longer promises and keener customers. The true cost of a promised minute is fixed in advance, so each reading can be checked against it.

What a broken promise costs, separately from slow food

The second number is how many customers a late minute loses. A cut of repeat rate by lateness cannot give it, because late orders are also slow ones: it blames lateness for the slowness too, and says the same whether customers punish broken promises or only slow food. Across a step, the promise moves by five minutes and the delivery does not, so the retention jump divided by the late-minutes jump (-3.4 minutes among orders slower than the shorter promise) is the cost of the broken promise alone. The simulation tests it on two kinds of customer, with retention per order drawn from each.

Retention here is an assumption for testing the estimator, not a finding: 60% order again after an on-time 30-minute delivery; the first kind of customer is 92% more responsive once late, the asymmetry Gui and Drerup report from Instacart orders. The steps need many orders because only the few near a step and slower than its shorter promise carry signal.

Does anything downstream spend the promise?

If dispatch, riders or kitchens read the promise, orders shown five more minutes take longer, and the promise shapes the outcome it predicts. The steps test it directly: delivery minutes should not jump at a step. A dispatcher that batches whenever every order is still expected by its promise does batch more of the orders shown the longer promise; the delivery-minutes jump shows how much that costs them.

Batching within the promise+0.52 min (+0.40 to +0.63)20%; +6.2 pts at a step (+5.7 pts to +6.8 pts)

The two readings are the inputs to a one-line rule. One more promised minute loses a share of orders equal to the elasticity and saves the cost of a late minute on every order that would have been late, so the promise is right when the chance of being late = elasticity × margin ÷ cost of a late minute. Here an order earns 8.6 AED before lateness costs, and a late minute costs 0.31 AED in refunds and contacts plus the customers it loses. Across the study's grid of assumptions the rule picks the simulation's winner in 6 of 9 cells and is within one step of it in 9.

Late orders become contacts; an AI agent answers them under a kernel

Every contact goes to the refund desk in Harness Ultra. The order record and refund history are trusted; the customer's message is not, and some messages exaggerate or carry an injected "system notice". Two scripted agents answer every contact: one follows the policy, one does whatever the customer asks. The kernel admits only what the published policy allows.

Forty deliveries from one simulated weeknight, green when on time and red when late, over the road network. Real routes take a median 1.8 times the straight-line time and 3.7 times at the 90th percentile, because crossing Sheikh Zayed Road means an interchange, and one-way loops make a trip and its reverse differ by about a quarter.

Assumptions

WhatAssumed
Checkout conversion30% at a 30-minute promise, falling 2% (relative) per extra minute; tested at 1% and 3%
Cost of lateness in future orders0.5% chance per late minute that a customer stops ordering, capped at 30%, times 250 AED; tested at half and double
Contacts3% of on-time orders; 10% plus 2 points per late minute, capped at 70%; 6 AED to handle
RefundsThe refund desk's published tiers: delivery fee from 10 minutes late, then 25%, 50% and 100% of the order from 20, 40 and 60 minutes, capped at 30, 60 and 120 AED
Revenue and rider cost18% commission plus a 7 AED fee; rider time at 0.45 AED a minute
DelaysRoad legs on the OpenStreetMap graph at motorbike speeds, stretched by traffic and rain using the level and tail width of a public delivery dataset (not its absolute times)
